# The Living Table North Star

Last updated: 2026-08-05

## Prime directive

**The whole reason The Living Table exists is to make D&D easier to track and easier to play from both the Dungeon Master and player perspectives.**

The product succeeds when the technology fades into the background and the group spends more time describing, deciding, roleplaying, rolling, and reacting—and less time searching books, rebuilding encounters, copying numbers, tracking scattered notes, or wondering what happens next.

The system remembers so the people at the table can play.

## What “easier for the DM” means

The DM should be able to:

- load a prepared adventure without rebuilding it;
- immediately understand the current Location, Room, situation, and likely next events;
- see every NPC, monster, hazard, clue, treasure, trigger, DC, consequence, and connected Room associated with the current area;
- read player-facing descriptions without exposing private notes;
- reveal information in one clear action;
- run NPCs and monsters from complete cards without opening another book;
- track initiative, HP, conditions, resources, room changes, quests, and treasure without maintaining separate notes;
- make a ruling, override automation, correct a mistake, and continue play quickly;
- save and resume without reconstructing the table;
- always know the most useful next action while remaining free to improvise.

The software supports the DM. It does not replace the DM, dictate the story, or force every ruling into automation.

## What “easier for the player” means

A player should be able to:

- understand what their character can do without studying a full rulebook during play;
- see attacks, spells, features, equipment, conditions, resources, and available actions together;
- understand the current revealed Location, Room, objective, creatures, clues, and consequences;
- describe an intended action before being forced into a specific button or skill;
- make common rolls and spend resources with minimal steps;
- see what changed after the DM resolves an action;
- follow initiative and know when their turn begins;
- reconnect without losing the character’s state;
- focus on decisions and roleplay rather than bookkeeping.

The player screen must remain a character station looking into the DM’s world—not a smaller copy of the DM dashboard.

## Product test for every feature

Before approving a feature, answer:

1. Which DM or player burden does this remove?
2. What book lookup, note, repeated entry, calculation, or navigation step becomes unnecessary?
3. Does it make the next useful action clearer?
4. Does it preserve the DM’s authority and the player’s freedom to describe intent?
5. Does it keep hidden information out of player data?
6. Does it reduce total cognitive load rather than merely moving bookkeeping to another screen?
7. Can a first-time user understand it from the interface and card wording?
8. Does it keep the group in the rhythm of play instead of interrupting the game?

A feature that adds more tracking than it removes must be redesigned or rejected.

## Information hierarchy

At any moment, the application should emphasize only what is useful now.

### DM priority

1. What the players perceive
2. What is secretly true
3. What the players might attempt
4. Which rule, roll, or DC applies
5. Success, failure, and conditional consequences
6. What changes in the Room or session
7. What should load, reveal, or happen next

### Player priority

1. What the character perceives
2. What the party is trying to accomplish
3. What the character can do now
4. What actions and resources remain
5. What roll or choice the DM requested
6. What result the DM revealed
7. What decision comes next

## Tracking contract

The authoritative session should eventually remember, without duplicate manual entry:

- current Location and Room;
- room discoveries, alterations, opened paths, disabled traps, and resolved events;
- revealed and hidden cards;
- quests, objectives, clues, and consequences;
- initiative, rounds, turns, held actions, reactions, and concentration;
- character HP, conditions, death saves, inspiration, spell slots, class resources, equipment, and item uses;
- monster and NPC HP, conditions, recharge abilities, morale, and encounter state;
- treasure ownership and transfers;
- DM rulings, overrides, undo history, and meaningful session events;
- campaign membership, claimed characters, ready state, save, reconnect, and resume.

The same fact should not need to be tracked independently in multiple modules.

## Interaction standards

- Prefer one obvious primary action over a row of equal-looking controls.
- Attach rules and consequences to the card feature that invokes them.
- Use plain language and familiar D&D terms.
- Automate setup and bookkeeping, not imagination or roleplay.
- Keep common actions fast; place advanced controls behind progressive disclosure.
- Preserve keyboard access, readable contrast, clear focus, and mobile usability.
- Confirm destructive actions and make ordinary corrections easy to undo.
- Never require the DM to reveal private information to operate the table.
- Never require a player to enter DM tools to manage a character.
- Never call a workflow intuitive until it has been observed in a real DM/player playtest.

## Definition of product success

A prepared one-shot is successful when a DM unfamiliar with the software can load it, invite players, understand the opening Room, present the scene, respond to player choices, run social interaction, exploration, and combat, track the session, and reach the ending without needing a second tracking system.

A player unfamiliar with the software should be able to claim a character, understand the character’s choices, follow the DM’s revealed game, take a turn, use features and items, and resume later without needing to learn the DM interface.

This North Star outranks visual novelty, feature count, technical cleverness, and marketplace expansion.
